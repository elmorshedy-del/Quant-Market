# revise-assumptions — change the frame, not the fudge factor

**Move:** List the assumptions that every current hypothesis shares. When evidence
keeps resisting all of them, the shared assumption is the prime suspect. Revise it.
Derive what the revised frame predicts *differently*, and test that difference.

## When to consider it

- Several rounds of refinement each fix one discrepancy and create another.
- Two results you trust contradict each other under the current definitions.
- An expected quantity, intermediate or effect has been looked for carefully and
  never found.
- The variables, units of analysis or target definition seem to fit the question badly:
  the "same" thing measured two ways disagrees.
- The research question itself may be ill-posed (e.g., asks for an average when
  the phenomenon is heterogeneous, or asks "whether" when it should ask "when").

## Actions

1. **Inventory the frame.** Write down every definition, measurement convention,
   unit of analysis, model class and background assumption the current hypotheses share.
   Mark which ones were tested and which were inherited.
2. **Locate the strain.** For each anomaly, note which shared assumption it would
   stop being anomalous under.
3. **Propose at most two revisions** (operational redefinition, a different representation or
   target variable, a different mechanism class). Keep the old frame as a live rival.
4. **Derive a discriminating prediction.** Find something the revised frame predicts
   that the old frame forbids, or the reverse. A revision that only re-describes known
   data has not been tested.
5. **Test, then update the blueprint.** If the revision wins, record in the ledger the
   change to the question, representation or plan, and why. Move the superseded frame to
   *Rejected explanations* with a `Revisit only if:` condition.

## Required outputs

- Assumption inventory, with tested and inherited assumptions marked.
- The revision, the discriminating prediction, and its result.
- An explicit ledger record of any change to the question, representation,
  mechanism or experimental plan.

## Failure checks

- Is the "revision" a free parameter in disguise that can absorb any result?
- Did you test the discriminating prediction, or only show that the new frame fits?
- Did the revision silently change the question so that it became easier to answer?
  Changing the question is allowed. Doing it silently is not.
- Was the old frame given a fair test before it was abandoned?

## Worked example — Albert Einstein (1905) and Peter Mitchell (1961)

Maxwell's electrodynamics and the principle that physics is the same in every
uniformly moving frame seemed to conflict, and ether-drift experiments kept finding
nothing. Rather than add another correction, Einstein questioned a definition that
everyone shared: simultaneity. He redefined it operationally, using light signals
exchanged between clocks. The conflict dissolved, and the revised frame made new,
testable predictions: time dilation and the relativistic mass–energy relation.

Biochemists spent years searching for the "high-energy chemical intermediate" that
was assumed to couple respiration to ATP synthesis, and never found it. Mitchell
dropped the assumption that the coupling was a chemical compound at all. He proposed
that respiration pumps protons across the mitochondrial membrane and that the
resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis. This predicted something the old frame did
not: an imposed pH gradient alone should make ATP. Jagendorf and Uribe (1966) soaked
chloroplasts in acid, transferred them to a basic buffer in the dark, and they made ATP.
